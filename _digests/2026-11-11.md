## Things I wrote {#wrote}

### [Some principles that have held up](https://x.com/mstockton/status/2119585033048510563)

The tools keep changing, but some of the practices have been fairly consistent for me. Describe what successful completion looks like without specifying every step. When the model keeps making the same mistake, write down the generalized principle, not another narrow rule. Find a chunk of work you can still review properly, and be willing to start over when a session goes off the rails. It's also worth rejecting some ideas. Being able to build something doesn't mean you should.

### [Give agents a way to check their work](https://x.com/mstockton/status/2118800599437439444)

I've been thinking more deeply about what correctness means for each project: if this were done correctly, what would it look like? That gets harder when the work is subjective or has a lot of nuance. But even having to think through the question is valuable. Building the checks early puts the agents in a much better position to do good work. For software, that includes a realistic environment where they can run the application, inspect logs, and use what they find to improve it.

### [Memory needs work between sessions too](https://x.com/mstockton/status/2118841080414134396)

I use a wiki for my projects and mine past sessions to distill learnings in a batch step. A lot of [Anthropic's article on memory](https://www.anthropic.com/research/components-of-agent-memory) matched that experience. Their “dreaming” process goes back through sessions and existing memories to correct mistakes and improve how the information is organized. I particularly liked that the wiki structure can change as the model learns more. There is still a lot to figure out, and sometimes searching the original sessions is useful alongside the wiki.

## Things I'm trying {#trying}

### [Exploring Shorewood's handwritten 1950 census records](https://shorewood1950.mattstockton.com/)

I spent a few hours with Fable 5.2 building an interactive explorer for my village's census records. You can search addresses and names, explore the data, and go back to the original handwritten lines. My [starting prompt](https://x.com/mstockton/status/2120219954058285487) was basically: find the handwritten records, build an interactive explorer, and ask me any questions.

What surprised me most was how well it handled the scanned PDFs. If you look at the handwriting and how the records relate to one another, some of it is hard for a human to understand. Fable figured out a lot for itself, often running around twenty subagents on different pieces of the work. I was also impressed by the visualizations and the user experience it came up with.

The transcriptions haven't been checked against every sheet, so the links back to the originals matter. As a final bit of polish, I had it make a [short film of a census taker working through a street](https://shorewood1950.mattstockton.com/assets/shorewood-1950-downer.mp4). I was pretty blown away by how much it could do with data that had previously felt too tedious to explore.

## Things I listened to {#listening}

### [Zach Lloyd - The 2026 Software Factory Standard](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LL0YmIyBKSU)

“Software factory” means a lot of different things to different people right now. I liked this episode because Lloyd lays out principles that could hold up across different implementations. A lot comes down to checking whether the work is correct and having improvements compound in the system itself.

- Warp saves agent runs and replays tasks across different models and harnesses. That lets them compare cost and correctness on their own work, rather than picking a setup from public benchmarks alone.
- Lloyd says their early runs cost about $80 per PR. Using an expensive model for triage and spawning too many subagents were two causes. Tuning the workflow brought that down to roughly $15 to $20.
- Because the workflow is defined in code, agents can propose changes to it based on recorded failures. The qualification is useful: replaying old runs to verify those proposed improvements was still something they wanted to build.

### [Kevin Roose - The Inside Story of the 50 Who Built Modern AI](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/the-every-podcast/id1719789201?i=1000799840102)

Roose describes using agents around a large reporting project while writing the book himself. There are some concrete ideas here for reviewing and navigating your own research.

- He gave chapter-review agents different perspectives: an AI skeptic, a technical historian, and an editor focused on the storytelling. Another agent brought their feedback together. He says this caught a couple of factual errors that he and his human editors had missed.
- He used NotebookLM to find who had told him a particular story and which sources to contact about an event. Finding the provenance of a detail is a different job from summarizing a pile of research.
- He separates “weightlifting,” where the effort is part of what you want, from “forklifting,” where you just need the work done. He kept the writing and used agents for research organization, review, and administrative work.

### [AI Daily Brief - AI Lurches Toward Recursive Self-Improvement](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/nlw/episodes/AI-Lurches-Toward-Recursive-Self-Improvement-e3rmekk)

A discussion of the weight-editing results, what people are paying for agents, and whether better models are enough to change how companies work.

- The math improvement in one weight-editing experiment came from fixing a reasoning-loop bug. The episode includes critiques that this is a bug fix, rather than evidence of a new general training method.
- Nathaniel Whittemore describes paying for work he would never have hired someone to do. The comparison includes the time to recruit and manage that person, not just their hourly rate.
- He argues that many companies use only a small part of the capability already available. Inertia and existing systems can hold adoption back even as the models improve.

More episodes and takeaways are in my [Listening Index](https://mattstockton.com/standalone/listening-index.html).

## Other things that caught my attention {#links}

### Posts

- [Karri Saarinen on organizations being overwhelmed from within](https://x.com/karrisaarinen/status/2117380299885817880): People can each produce work they think is useful while collectively changing the company's direction faster than anyone can follow. He asks how leaders keep understanding what is happening without trying to review everything.
- [Cloudflare's security-audit skill](https://github.com/cloudflare/security-audit-skill): A reusable starting point for auditing a repository, including agents that try to disprove candidate findings. It distinguishes confirmed issues from rejected ones and those that still need validation.
- [Brian Scanlan's five-whys skill](https://github.com/intercom/2x-skills/blob/main/plugins/incident-tools/skills/five-whys/SKILL.md): An incident-analysis workflow that checks facts against primary evidence, then challenges the causal chain. It asks what observation would confirm or refute an inference, rather than letting a plausible explanation become the accepted one.

### Articles

- [How Odessia built with agents](https://x.com/FDavidsonT/status/2120214953349263473): The team's account covers shared context, voice prompts, turning repeated corrections into skills, and using agents across the business. I [called it a blueprint for going from zero to one](https://x.com/mstockton/status/2120240306121830606). It also describes the human effort involved: more output meant more decisions and a more intense pace.
- [Shopify's app rebuild](https://shopify.engineering/shopify-app-rebuild): Thirty engineers rebuilt more than 300 screens in twelve weeks. Their account gets into the work around the agents: fast tests without launching a device, small reviewable changes, and turning review comments into updated instructions. They used the existing app as the reference; they explicitly leave open how this changes for new features.
- [Simon Willison tries interpreting model weights on his laptop](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/3/qwen-can-interpret-model-weights/): He reproduces part of the weight-reading experiment with Qwen, identifying three of four games from their networks' weights. He separates that result from experiments where a model uses code to modify another model, and from the much bigger predictions circulating around both.
- [Thinking about investment research as a computational graph](https://x.com/fyxlong/status/2119840676242456609): A practitioner at Coatue describes breaking investment work into smaller functions that can be evaluated separately. The objective is performance, so some steps may be better done with code, statistical methods, or people. Choosing the steps and how they connect remains a substantial part of the work.
